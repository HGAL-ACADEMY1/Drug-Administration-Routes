Welcome to this lecture! 🎓 Right now, we shall explore a fascinating journey: the journey of medicine through the human body. 💊

Think of taking a drug like sending a package to a friend. The Route of Administration is simply the shipping method you choose. Are you sending it by air mail? By sea? Or are you hand-delivering it?

The method we choose depends on two things:
1. What the package is made of (is the drug water-soluble or lipid-soluble?).
2. How fast we need it to get there (do we need immediate relief, or long-term treatment?).

There are two main highways we can take: Enteral (using the gut) and Parenteral (skipping the gut).

Okay, let's now move on to the first highway: Enteral. 🛣️

The most famous one here is the Oral Route. This is when you swallow a pill. It’s very common, but it takes a complicated path! 🗺️

When you swallow a pill, it goes to your stomach, but the real magic happens in the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine). Why? because it has a huge surface area to soak up the drug.

But there is a catch! ✋ Before the drug gets to the rest of the body, it has to pass through a security checkpoint called the Liver via the portal circulation. This is called First-Pass Metabolism.

Imagine the liver is a strict security guard that confiscates some of the drug before letting it pass. This limits how effective the drug is. Also, if you have food in your stomach, it can slow things down, and stomach acid can actually destroy some drugs (like Ampicillin). 🍔

To fix this, we sometimes use an Enteric Coating on pills to protect them from that harsh acid.

Now, let's talk about the Sublingual Route. This is when you place a pill under your tongue. 👅

This is a smart shortcut! The drug goes directly into the blood capillaries under your tongue. It completely bypasses the liver and the intestine. No security checkpoint! 🚀

Then there is the Rectal Route. It sounds a bit awkward, but it’s very useful. About 50% of the drug given this way skips the liver. It’s perfect for patients who are vomiting and can’t swallow a pill. 🤢

Okay, let’s now move on to the second highway: Parenteral. 💉

This route is used when we need the drug to work FAST (like in an emergency), or if the patient is unconscious. The three big ones are IV, IM, and SC.

Intravenous (I.V): This is the injection directly into a vein. It is the most common parenteral route. Because it goes straight into the blood, it skips the stomach and liver completely. The drug is 100% available to the body immediately.

But be careful! ⚠️ Once you inject it, you can't take it back (unlike vomiting up a pill). You must be very clean (sterile) to avoid infections or bad reactions.

Intramuscular (I.M): This is an injection into the muscle. If the drug is in water (aqueous), it absorbs fast. If it's in an oil or suspension (depot preparation), it absorbs slowly over time.

Subcutaneous (SC): This is injected just under the skin. It absorbs slower than IV. We use this for things like insulin or contraceptive implants (like Levonorgestrel) that need to work for a long time.

Now that we understand the main routes, let's talk about some special ones! 🌟

💨 Inhalation: Like a Ventolin inhaler for asthma. It works very fast because the lungs have a huge surface area.

👃 Intranasal: Through the nose, like nasal sprays for a stuffy nose.

🧠 Intrathecal / Intraventricular: This is serious business. Drugs are put directly into the spinal fluid or brain fluid. We do this for severe infections like cryptococcal meningitis.

🧴 Topical: Creams applied to the skin for local problems (like a rash). It usually stays on the skin.

🩹 Transdermal: This is a patch on the skin, but the drug is designed to go through the skin into the blood to work on the whole body. An example is a Nitroglycerine patch for heart pain (angina).

Let's now dive into Absorption. 🧽

Absorption is just the transfer of the drug from where you put it, into the bloodstream. Remember: IV administration has complete absorption (100%). All other routes are partial.

So, how does a drug move from your gut to your blood? It uses Transport. 🚚

1. Passive Diffusion: Imagine a crowd of people pushing into an empty room. Drugs move from High Concentration to Low Concentration. No energy is needed. Most drugs do this.

2. Active Transport: This is like pushing a cart uphill. It needs Energy (ATP) and a special carrier protein to move the drug against the natural flow (Low to High concentration).

At this point, it's important to consider the pH (acidity). 🧪

Most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases. Here is the golden rule: Uncharged drugs pass through membranes easily. Charged drugs do not.

For a weak Acid (HA), the uncharged form (HA) can pass, but the charged part (A-) cannot.

HA ⇌ H+ + A-

For a weak Base, the uncharged Base (B) passes, but the charged form (BH+) gets stuck.

BH+ ⇌ B + H+

To figure out how much drug is on either side of a membrane, we use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. Don't worry, here is the simplified version:

For Acids:
pH = pKa + Log [A-] / [HA]

For Bases:
pH = pKa + Log [B] / [BH+]

Next, we’ll look at Physical Factors influencing absorption. 🩸

1. Blood Flow: More blood flow = Better absorption. (The intestine has more blood than the stomach).

2. Surface Area: Bigger area = Better absorption. (The intestine is 1000x bigger than the stomach!).

3. Contact Time: If the drug moves too fast (like when you have diarrhea), it won't be absorbed well. It needs time to soak in.

Now, let's define a very important word: Bioavailability. 📉

This is the fraction of the drug that actually makes it to your blood circulation unchanged. We calculate this by looking at the "Area Under the Curve" (AUC) in a blood test graph.

F (Bioavailability) = AUC(oral) / AUC(IV)

Factors affecting this include how sick the patient is, the chemical properties of the drug, and the form of the drug (pill vs. liquid).

Two drugs are Bioequivalent if they have the same bioavailability. They are Therapeutic Equivalents if they cure the patient equally well.

Let's move to Drug Metabolism. 🏭

The body needs to eventually get rid of the drug. The Liver is the main factory for this. Sometimes, we give an inactive drug (Pro-drug) and the liver actually activates it!

Metabolism usually follows First-Order Kinetics. This means a constant percentage of the drug is removed per hour (e.g., 50% every 4 hours).

Rate of metabolism ∝ [Concentration of Drug]

However, some drugs like Aspirin follow Zero-Order Kinetics. This happens when the enzymes are full (saturated). The body removes a constant amount (e.g., 100mg per hour), no matter how much you took.

Finally, let's look at Elimination. 🚽

The Kidney is the main exit door (via urine).
1. Glomerular Filtration: Free drug flows into the kidney filters.
2. Tubular Secretion: Some drugs are actively pumped into the urine.
3. Tubular Re-absorption: If a drug is lipid-soluble, it might try to sneak back into the blood!

Pro Tip: 💡 If someone takes an overdose of Phenobarbital (a weak acid), we can give them Bicarbonate. This makes the urine alkaline (basic), which charges the drug (ionizes it). Remember? Charged drugs can't pass membranes! So, the drug gets trapped in the urine and peed out. That saves the patient!

And that brings us to the end of our journey through the body! 🏁
